Getting cross-track error right on WGS-84

Created on November 02, 2026 by Keyan Ding

2026   ·   Engineering Notes

A spherical estimate, a missing sign, and what 1,320 test cases taught me about turning geometry into code.

Given two waypoints and an aircraft position, I wanted the signed distance from the aircraft to the geodesic through those waypoints. The projection could fall before the start or beyond the end.

The geometry fits on a page, but the implementation needed much more care about direction, stopping conditions, and what an error measurement actually means.

A sphere makes a useful first estimate

On a sphere, the route lies in a plane through the Earth's center. A cross product gives the plane's normal; projecting the aircraft's unit vector onto that plane and normalizing it gives a nearby point on the great circle.

Earth is flattened at the poles. WGS-84 includes an ellipsoidal Earth model, so a spherical projection generally differs from the ellipsoidal one. I used the spherical result as the initial estimate. For this implementation I used the direct and inverse geodesic formulas in Appendix E of RTCA DO-283C, the minimum operational performance standard for RNP area-navigation equipment. The direct formula determines the endpoint and final bearing from a known starting point, initial bearing, and distance, while the inverse formula determines the distance and bearings between two known points.

The solver

Let Q(s) be the point reached by traveling signed distance s from the start along the route. Negative distances extend behind the start. At Q(s), let α be the route's forward tangent bearing and β the bearing toward the aircraft. I solve the local perpendicularity condition:

f(s) = cosd(β(s) − α(s)) = 0

Here, cosd takes degrees. A point coincident with the aircraft is handled directly because its bearing to the aircraft is undefined. With this and other edge cases addressed, the following steps proceed to compute the remaining bearings and distances:

  1. Compute the spherical estimate P₀, then convert its distance from the start into a signed initial distance s₀.
  2. Evaluate the actual ellipsoidal point Q(s₀). Accept it if the residual magnitude is at most 1e-4.
  3. Otherwise, start at s₀ ± 185 m. Use secant interpolation, replace the endpoint with the same residual sign to ensure convergence, and use the midpoint if the denominator is zero or interpolation leaves the interval.
  4. Stop on the residual tolerance or a width of 0.001 m. After 20 iterations, return the remaining interval's midpoint and record that fallback separately.

This is the update procedure in which the endpoints bracket a root. To improve robustness, the interval can be expanded when necessary so that the root remains enclosed by the current bracket throughout the iterations, thereby helping preserve the convergence guarantee.

An unsigned initial distance

An inverse geodesic returns a nonnegative distance, but my along-route coordinate also needs a sign to indicate which side of the segment’s reference point the projected point lies on. For example, when an aircraft is projected near the start of a route segment, the left bracket of the secant method may extend before the segment start. Without a signed distance, a projection 100 km behind the start would be indistinguishable from one 100 km ahead, and could therefore be interpreted as an estimate in the wrong direction.

I determine the sign from the angle between the start-to-P₀ bearing and the route's initial bearing. If their cosine is negative, I negate the distance. This selects the backward direction for the local initial estimate.

To isolate the effect, I reran test case 820 with only that correction disabled. The route runs east along the equator for 100 km. The aircraft is 10 km south of a reference foot 100 km before the start.

The unsigned interval never contained the foot, but it still became narrow enough to stop, returning a cross-track distance of −200.435 km instead of −10 km. Restoring the sign let the initial residual check accept the correct foot without iteration. This shows that a small interval is useful evidence only when it encloses the intended solution.

Other details that changed the result

For the left/right sign, I initially considered the bearing from the projection to the destination waypoint. However, beyond the destination, that bearing points backward; at the destination itself, it is undefined. Reusing the forward tangent bearing from the direct geodesic calculation fixes both cases. I also maintain one angle convention throughout: [−180°, 180°).

The tolerances have different units. A residual of 1e-4 is dimensionless; an interval width of 0.001 m is a distance. In one test, the initial residual passed even though the foot was 1.831 m from the reference. Its cross-track distance error was only about 0.091 mm. Accepting a small residual does not sacrifice much accuracy in the projected coordinates.

The exit counts are 127 + 1,180 + 13 = 1,320. The distance threshold here means interval width, not cross-track distance error. The width check runs first; cases 614, 615, 623, and 624 also met the residual tolerance at the returned midpoint. The 354 initial-residual exits did not construct an interval, and the 13 endpoint-coincidence exits bypassed both checks. The results above use the fixed ±185 m interval without expansion.

Passing meant distance error below 2 mm, position error below 2 m, correct signs for nonzero offsets, and at most 20 iterations. The reference geometry shares the direct solver, so these are consistency tests. Seven standard reference cases and 30 independent geodesic ODE comparisons provide separate checks on the geodesic routines.

What this experiment taught me

The entire operating envelope should be treated as the scope for algorithm validation: different latitudes, longitude crossings, and aircraft positions whose projections fall before, within, or beyond the route segment. A useful test suite also needs points on the route and extremely close to it. Ordinary mid-segment examples would have missed several of the problems here.

The secant formula is simple to implement, but details matter. For this interval-based variant, I verified that the initial endpoints had opposite signs, endpoint updates preserved that sign change, and successive intervals became smaller. These checks do not guarantee rapid convergence: one endpoint can remain fixed while progress slows. Convergence speed therefore needs its own checks against the iteration budget.

The objective function needs careful design too. Near the route, the bearing toward the aircraft can change sharply as the candidate foot passes it. On the route itself, that bearing is undefined at coincidence, and the cosine residual can jump between opposite signs rather than smoothly reach zero. A residual-only stopping rule is therefore insufficient: coincidence handling and a justified positional stopping condition matter too. The secant denominator also needs its own zero check.

Ranges and units should be consistent throughout. I would make angle ranges, bearing directions, signed distances, and units explicit at every interface. I would also choose tolerances from the required output accuracy, then measure function evaluations and runtime: tighter projection coordinates may cost more without materially improving cross-track distance. The 20-iteration limit bounds the outer loop, not total execution time or error. Every exit should say whether it met a tolerance or merely exhausted its budget.
